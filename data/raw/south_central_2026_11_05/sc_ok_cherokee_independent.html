Cherokee Nation language immersion school now independent

  • BY CHAD HUNTER & D. SEAN ROWLEY Senior Reporters
  • Jun 29, 2026
  • Jun 29, 2026 Updated Jun 29, 2026

TAHLEQUAH – Amendments to the Durbin Feeling Language Preservation Act that shift full oversight of the Cherokee Nation Immersion School to the tribe were passed and signed into law June 25.

Taught fully in the Cherokee language, the school, founded in 2001, had been part of the state charter program for 16 years.

“Our declaration of independence today is not because we have some list of grievances with the state of Oklahoma,” Principal Chief Chuck Hoskin Jr. said. “We’ve decided that if we are going to revitalize our language, we have to do it. We have to have our system of accountability. That’s why the amendments today set up a new board of education – an advisory board made up of fluent speakers, made up of education professionals – to oversee our own affairs.”

Hoskin signed the legislation during a special ceremony in Tahlequah at the Durbin Feeling Language Center, where the Immersion School is located. Earlier that same day, members of the Tribal Council voted 14-0 in favor of full oversight by the tribe. The vote comes on the heels of Chief Hoskin ending the school’s charter agreement under authority the Council previously granted him.

“Today is us exercising our sovereignty again,” Chief of Staff Corey Bunch said. “We’re taking charge of what we’re doing in our Cherokee language school.”

The changes call for third-party accreditation, sought through the World Indigenous Nations Higher Education Consortium rather than the Oklahoma State Department of Education. And while ending the state charter is not cost free, Hoskin said the impact will be manageable. Attendant state and federal funding linked to the charter amount to roughly $2.5 million annually. Hoskin said the Nation has the financial latitude to handle the extra expense.

“We have to decide if the addition to our budget ... is worth the expression of tribal sovereignty and self-governance that this represents,” Hoskin said. “I think unquestionably it is.”

The Cherokee Nation accepted its state charter in 2010 under the leadership of then-Principal Chief Chad Smith.

“I think at the time, it was the right decision because it enabled us to access some resources and launch immersion education,” Hoskin said. “Thanks to the Durbin Feeling Language Preservation Act of 2019, we’re now looking back across seven years of $175 million invested in our language.”

The act was reauthorized in 2024 with a minimum operating fund of $18 million annually. The funding level was $28 million in 2025.

“The Durbin Feeling Language Act recognizes that preserving our language requires action, not just good intentions,” Tribal Councilor and second-language speaker Codey Poindexter said. “It creates pathways for new speakers while honoring our first-language speakers who continue to guide us.”

Cherokee Nation Immersion School classes are taught exclusively in the Cherokee language, both written and spoken. The Sequoyah syllabary is used for all print materials.

“This is one of the most incredible programs around anywhere in any effort to revitalize a Native language,” Hoskin said. “You can go into this building, and you can see Cherokee babies that are hearing the first words they’ve ever heard in their lives in our language. That hasn’t happened on this scale in a long, long time. It’s a beautiful thing to see.”
