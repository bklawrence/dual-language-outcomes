Blue Valley families question Chinese Immersion cut as district warns of $10M shortfall

Blue Valley officials have warned budget issues could result in more reductions
By Ale Espinosa
Published: Oct. 15, 2026 at 5:14 PM CDT
Add Us On GoogleAdd as a preferred source on Google

OVERLAND PARK, Kan. (KCTV) - Blue Valley families who chose the district for its reputation for excellence say they learned just an hour before a community roundtable that the Chinese Immersion program will end next year.

District leaders say the program is one of several reductions needed to close a $10 million budget gap. They say they will not announce additional cuts in athletics, the arts and classes until impacted staff are notified first.

Board President Jan Kessinger tells KCTV5 the district is financially sound but faces what he calls a perfect storm of state underfunding for special education, declining enrollment and rising costs.

“Blue Valley is financially sound, but we do face a real crisis. It’s a perfect storm,” Kessinger said.

Kylie Frederick, a Blue Valley graduate, has two boys in immersion at Wolf Springs - a sixth grader and a seventh grader who entered through the lottery in 2019.

Frederick said the district asked families to commit through high school, creating what she described as a second family.

“The district had asked us to make it a commitment for our families, which we were totally willing to do,” Frederick said. “It created a community, almost like a second family.”

She said 90% of PTO parent involvement at Wolf Springs comes from immersion families and worries about the impact on both the elementary and middle school community.

Joe Arthur moved his family back to the Kansas City area from Boston for Blue Valley schools. His son Atticus just started kindergarten in immersion.

‘I moved here for it’: Blue Valley to eliminate Chinese Immersion program

“We ultimately settled on Blue Valley because of everything that we’ve read. It was top 1% of schools,” Arthur said. “We said, ‘Oh, he’s going to get a top-tier education. And he’s going to be set.’”

Arthur said the family pays more in housing and taxes to live in the district, a tradeoff he compared to private school costs, and drives 10-to-15 minutes each way to Wolf Springs. He said he was willing to make that drive because the program seemed worth it.

Both families said enrollment was presented as a path through high school with no warning about funding uncertainty.

“They said this is gonna be a commitment... all the way to high school... nobody ever mentioned we may not have funding,” Arthur said.

“If we had known that there was even the slightest bit of uncertainty, we probably wouldn’t have done that,” he added.

Kessinger, a former state representative, said the shortfall is driven by special education costs the state is required to cover.

“In the last 15 years, we’ve had to transfer $124 million out of the general fund,” Kessinger said. “This year, it’s been $18.9 million.”

Kansas law requires the state to cover 92% of excess special education costs, district leaders say. Kessinger said lawmakers have not met that obligation.

“92% of our excess funding for special ed... needs to be appropriated by the legislature. And they have not done it,” he said.

According to Blue Valley’s website and meeting records, state aid has fallen short of that 92% requirement for more than a decade.

The district’s Finance page and School Funding 101 breakdown show the district relies on a mix of state, federal, local and family-paid funds, with $144 million in state aid for staffing and $36 million in state special education grants.

Kessinger said administration was cut first.

“Our first cuts were in administration. We cut over $6 million,” Kessinger said. “Administrators got zero [raise]. We were able to give the teachers a 1% raise... now we’re at the point where it is going to impact students.”

He said no schools will close, unlike in neighboring Olathe, but reductions are coming.

“We are not closing schools... We are reducing certain areas, whether it’s certain areas in athletics, certain areas in the arts, certain classes,” Kessinger said.

Johnson County districts have joined together to pressure the state to meet its obligation, Kessinger said, and litigation remains possible.

“Whether that’s a lawsuit, that remains to be seen,” he said. “We hope that the legislature, especially the new one that’s elected in November, will be able to say we need to properly fund schools.”

The district last week posted a budget update video on YouTube outlining the challenges behind this round of cuts.

Frederick said her seventh grader, for whom Chinese is his favorite part of the day, is devastated. Her sixth grader worked hard to catch up in the language, she said, making an abrupt end especially difficult.

“Chinese did not come easy to him. So it has been a lot of labor to get him to where he’s at today,” she said.

Frederick said the family drives daily to Wolf Springs and would have continued to do so to stay in the program. She said the family will likely stay in the area but is no longer ruling out a move to Louisburg or farther south for more space - a move previously set aside to stay in the immersion program.

Arthur said he has not yet told Atticus, who has already switched schools twice after two daycare closures.

“He’s gonna be confused why we have to switch schools yet again,” Arthur said. “It’s a tough pill for a little kid.”

Arthur said he would consider moving schools within the district to keep the program, or paying more to sustain it, and questioned the cost breakdown given similar class sizes of 26 to 28 students.

“What is it about Chinese immersion that’s costing this extra amount of money? And what can we do to fix that?” Arthur said. “I would be willing to make additional sacrifices, whether that’s monetary.”

“If this can’t get fixed, I would probably just move back to Boston,” he said.
