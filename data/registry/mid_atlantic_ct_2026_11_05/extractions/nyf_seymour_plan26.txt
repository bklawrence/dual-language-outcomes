The Continuation Plan serves as a blueprint to comprehensively plan for the continuum of improvement moving from the completion of one school year into the next. • School- and district-based decisions for 2026-2027 should be made using a wide range of reliable data sources while examining outcomes from the 2025-2026 school year. • Decisions should be rooted in evidence based on the results of the completed needs assessment and aligned with district goals for continuous improvement. • The plan should include a clear focus on how data and evidence will guide the selection and implementation of Lead Strategies, how these strategies will be monitored for impact, as well as how teaching and learning will be supported via district systems, structures and resources to ensure Demonstrable Improvement Indicator (DII) target attainment. Directions The Continuation Plan will be organized around Lead Strategies and systems and/or structures to support those strategies. Structures are the intentional routines and practices that strengthen a school’s core Tier 1 instructional program for teaching and learning in daily work. Strong structures ensure that effective practices and standards aligned instruction are consistent across the school, clear to all staff, and sustained even as leadership or initiatives change. Improvement, in this frame, is not about launching new programs but about strengthening the systems, structures, and habits that enable great teaching and learning to happen reliably, every day. This Continuation Plan should demonstrate how leadership is driving continuous improvement and aligning efforts to achieve the school’s Demonstrable Improvement (DI) Indicator targets. To build this work on a sustainable foundation, schools must ground their planning in the core components of an Integrated Multi-Tiered System of Supports (MTSS-I). This improvement plan focuses specifically on establishing and strengthening the systems and structures that ensure high-quality Tier 1 instruction is intentionally designed and delivered in every learning space. School leaders and improvement planning teams are strongly encouraged to review the MTSS-I Framework published by NYSED to deepen their understanding of a comprehensive MTSS-I system: NYSED MTSS-I Pilot Framework (October 2023). Additional information and useful resources for MTSS-I are available through the American Institutes for Research. Within each part of this plan template, school and district leadership should provide a concise summary of each lead strategy and the structures that will be developed to implement, support, and monitor the progress of those strategies. 2 After reflecting on and analyzing the results of the completed needs assessment and outcomes of the prior year’s improvement plan, the Continuation Plan should include: • Systems, structures or processes to be used to monitor fidelity of the implementation of Lead Strategies. • Actions to measure impact inclusive of data, observations, and/or stakeholder feedback. • Resource allocation to implement the plan with fidelity. • Adjustments made in response to findings to strengthen outcomes. The summary should be written in terms easily understood by the community-at-large. Please avoid terms and acronyms that are unfamiliar to the public. Limit the summary to no more than 500 words. All links included in the summary must be made publicly accessible prior to submitting the plan. Seymour Dual Language Academy currently serves 553 students in grades PreK-5. Over 50% of the student population is identified as English as a New Language Learner. The school enter Year 1 of Receivership status following a designation of Comprehensive Support and Improvement due to historical underperformance in Level 1 and Level 2 ELA and mathematics data. What do we know about this school that needs to be addressed to better serve the learner community? To better serve our community, Seymour must address a significant gap between instructional intent and student experience, consistency across expectations, proactive attendance follow-up, and the effectiveness of Professional Learning communities (PLCs) in driving classroom implementation. After reviewing survey data from students, families, and staff, several consistent themes emerged that highlight both strengths and areas for growth at Seymour Dual Language Academy. Across all stakeholder groups, there is a strong and shared perception that the school fosters a supportive, inclusive, and welcoming environment. Students report feeling supported, held to expectations, and motivated to attend school regularly. Teachers believe the school helps every student feel seen, valued, and part of the community, while families indicate that their children feel known and welcomed at school. 3 Despite these strengths, a key area of concern across all groups is the clarity and consistency of expectations. Families express less confidence that students are consistently treated fairly or held to high expectations. Teachers acknowledge challenges in ensuring expectations are clearly communicated and applied consistently. Students report receiving mixed messages about what it means to work hard and produce high-quality work, suggesting a need for greater alignment in messaging and practice. What common themes emerge across attendance data, student voice, observations, and climate surveys regarding belonging? Attendance and follow-up practices also emerged as an area for improvement. Students are unsure whether someone will check in when they are absent, and families report inconsistent outreach following absences. Teachers note that while attendance is monitored, follow-up often occurs only after patterns of concern develop, rather than through consistent and proactive systems. Notably, 19% of students report that no one checks in with them after they have been absent, indicating a gap in student support systems. What is the most important student learning challenge that appears across multiple data sources? What strengths can you leverage? Perceptions of academic rigor and instructional impact are mixed across stakeholder groups. While teachers believe that instructional tasks promote higher-order thinking, students report fewer consistent opportunities to think deeply, explain their reasoning, and learn from mistakes. Families echo these mixed perceptions, suggesting that while the intention for rigorous instruction exists, its implementation is not consistently experienced by students. Differences in perspective also point to a gap between instructional intent and student experience. Teachers describe a shared understanding of high-quality instruction within PLCs; however, 17% of teachers strongly disagree that systems prevent students from “falling through the cracks,” indicating ongoing concerns about student support. Additionally, students report limited opportunities for critical thinking and productive struggle, reinforcing that collaborative planning is not yet consistently translating into classroom practice or improved student outcomes. What specific, high-leverage skills or misunderstandings are limiting students’ ability to progress? Across our reading and math diagnostic data (NWEA and DIBELS) and classroom evidence, the highest-leverage needs are foundational reading skills and academic vocabulary in both English and Spanish, and the ability to explain reasoning in mathematics. Because more than half of our students are learning English as a new language, limited opportunities to speak, write, and reason using academic language slow progress in every subject. In addition, students report receiving mixed messages about what it means to produce high-quality work, so some students equate finishing quickly with working hard. Strengthening language development, setting clear expectations for quality work, and providing regular opportunities to think deeply and learn from mistakes will unlock progress across content areas. 4 Which aspects of family and community partnership appear to influence attendance, engagement, behavior, or academic outcomes? Family partnership most directly influences attendance and engagement. Families report that outreach after student absences has been inconsistent, and 19% of students say no one checks in when they miss school; when follow-up does occur, students report a stronger sense of belonging and greater motivation to attend. Our needs assessment also identified the partnership among the school, families, and community organizations as an area requiring stronger coordination, so that academic, behavioral, social-emotional, and attendance supports reach families in a timely way. Two-way communication in families’ home languages and well-coordinated community partnerships are the aspects most likely to improve attendance, engagement, and academic outcomes. Overall, the findings suggest that while Seymour has established a strong foundation of belonging and community, there is a need to strengthen consistency across expectations, attendance follow-up, and instructional practices. In particular, enhancing the effectiveness of PLCs—through deeper data analysis and a stronger connection between planning and classroom implementation—will be critical to ensuring that all students experience rigorous, equitable, and high-quality instruction every day. These findings shape the four Lead Strategies in this plan, which work together as one system: a single building-wide dual-language curriculum gives teachers a common foundation; collaborative teacher teams use that foundation to plan cognitively demanding tasks for every student; proactive attendance and belonging supports ensure students are present and connected to that instruction; and the community school model equips families and community partners to reinforce the same goals at home. Vision for High Quality Learning: Where Do We Want to Be? Tier 1 learning at Seymour Dual Language Academy is grounded in a respectful and joyful environment where all students feel valued, What do we expect Tier 1 safe, and motivated to engage. Classrooms are conducive and learning to look and feel language-rich, designed to foster communication, collaboration, and like in every classroom? a strong sense of belonging. 5 Instruction is centered on grade-level content that is both rigorous and accessible, with clear access points to ensure all learners can engage meaningfully. Students regularly participate in independent and group practice, building both confidence and collaboration skills. Teachers provide modeling and guided practice to support understanding before releasing responsibility to students. Learning experiences promote student ownership, with a balance of teacher facilitation and student voice. Classrooms emphasize accountable talk, where students explain their thinking, listen to peers, and engage in meaningful discussions. Opportunities for risk- taking and productive struggle are intentionally built into instruction, allowing students to deepen their understanding and learn from mistakes. Impact on Learning - Engage in productive struggle as they work through challenging tasks and problems. - Participate in rich, high-level discussions and academic conversations that promote critical thinking and deeper understanding. - Collaborate with peers to explore ideas, justify reasoning, and learn from multiple perspectives. - Demonstrate understanding and mastery of content through meaningful application of knowledge and skills. - Develop increasing independence and confidence as learners through the gradual transfer of responsibility from teacher to student. In daily learning, students experience choice and variety: they may learn with their teacher in a small group, collaborate with classmates, or practice independently — including with technology — and they set goals, monitor their own progress, and choose how to show what they have learned. Across all classrooms, students are expected to think critically, communicate effectively, and actively engage in their learning every day.
