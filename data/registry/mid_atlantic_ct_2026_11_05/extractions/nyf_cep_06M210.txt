Collaboratively developed by the School Leadership Team (SLT) in accordance with the requirements of Chancellor’s Regulation A-655, the CEP meets New York State Education Department (NYSED) requirements under the Every Student Succeeds Act (ESSA) and aligns with NYC Public Schools’ (NYCPS) system-wide priorities. By engaging all stakeholders—school leaders, teachers, families, students and community members—schools can create a data-informed, equity- focused roadmap for student success and continuous improvement. Schools should focus their CEP on the five system-wide priorities below, connecting their planning to the data discussions and conversations with stakeholders held as part of the school’s needs assessment: Priority 1: All students learn to read well Priority 2: All students are physically and emotionally safe Priority 3: All students have a high-quality academic experience Priority 4: All students graduate college and career ready and have a strong plan and pathway to economic security Priority 5: All districts and schools are more inclusive and responsive for parents and families, including having more families choose NYC Public Schools The Education Planning Process The CEP follows an equity-driven, continuous improvement cycle, rooted in the system-wide priorities. This begins with a comprehensive needs assessment of the school’s most current data to identify priority needs and root causes. Next, the SLT establishes specific, measurable, attainable, relevant, and timely— SMART goal(s) aligned to each priority and determines targets to measure progress toward meeting the annual goals. The SLT and instructional leaders define key evidence-based key strategies, professional learning activities and parent, family and community empowerment efforts. They monitor the implementation of those strategies and activities and track progress through measurable targets. Then, the SLT aligns the school-based budget with human and instructional resources to support action plans. These include evidence based instructional strategies that address the educational needs of historically underserved populations and tackle structural inequities. Schools also address the needs of Students with Disabilities (SWD) through the High Quality Inclusive & Accessible Learning for Students with Disabilities section of the plan. SLTs also develop a plan to improve attendance and reduce chronic absenteeism. The Multi-tiered Systems of Support (MTSS) framework, organizes supports into three tiers: Tier 1 provides schoolwide, high-quality programming for all students, including SWDs; Tier 2 offers data informed small-group interventions for at-promise students; and Tier 3 delivers intensive, individualized support for students with persistent needs. This facilitates the strategic planning and necessary resource alignment to ensure their school community appropriately and adequately serves all students well, in partnership with students and families. MTSS identifies the academic support services to be provided and ensures that students receive Response to Intervention (RtI) and Positive Behavioral Interventions and Support (PBIS) strategies as needed. See NYSED’s memo to view the criteria for determining AIS services. A culturally responsive-sustaining approach to intervention embeds culturally relevant texts and historical narratives into literacy and social studies interventions, ensuring students receive the support that reflects their identities and lived experiences, increasing engagement and achievement. Explanation/Background: Title I Schoolwide Program schools are expected to use the flexibility available to them to integrate services and programs with the aim of enhancing the entire educational program and helping all students reach proficient and advanced levels of achievement. In addition to coordinating and integrating services, Schoolwide Program schools may combine most Federal, State and local funds to provide those services. By consolidating funds from Federal, State, and local sources, a Schoolwide Program school can address its needs using all its available resources. This gives a school more flexibility in how it uses available resources to meet the identified needs of all its students. Consolidating funds in a Schoolwide Program means that a school treats the funds it is consolidating like they are a single “pool” of funds. In other words, the funds from the contributing programs in the school lose their individual identity and the school has one flexible pool of funds. The school uses funds from this consolidated Schoolwide pool to support any activity of the Schoolwide Program without regard to which program contributed the specific funds used for a particular activity – so long as the use of funds meets the intent and purposes of the programs from which the funds originate. To consolidate funding in a Schoolwide Program, the school does not literally need to combine funds in a single account or pool with its own accounting code. Rather, the word “pool” is used conceptually to convey that a Schoolwide Program school has the use of all consolidated funds available to it for the dedicated function of operating a Schoolwide Program without regard to the identity of those funds. Consolidating Federal funds in a Schoolwide Program has the following additional advantages: Consolidating Federal funds eases the requirements for accounting for funds from each specific program separately, because a Schoolwide school is not required to distinguish among funds received from different sources when accounting for their use. A school that consolidates Federal funds in its Schoolwide Program is not required to meet most of the statutory and regulatory requirements of the specific Federal programs included in the consolidation (e.g., semi-annual time and effort reporting for Title I). However, the school must ensure that it meets the intent and purposes of the Federal programs included in the consolidation so that the needs of the intended beneficiaries are met. 2. English as a New Language (ENL) Stand-alone ENL: Stand-alone ENL students groups are formed based on the NYSITELL, NYSESLAT and RLAT English level results, and are comprised by "ungraded, heterogenous, and homogenous" groups. These students are served by Ms. Isabel Lobelo, in a separate classroom, and Mr. Miguel Guity for elementary school in a separate classroom as well. Entering/Emerging: students begin learning the English language structure, i.e., alphabet, letter-sound recognition, vowels, sight words, (phonemic awareness) reinforced with repetition, grade and level-appropriate worksheets to slowly build up the acquisition of the second language, scaffolding instruction in the student’s home language. We also use TPR so the students can answer questions using one or two words. Emphasis is on repetition and breaking down of sounds to acquire pronunciation and comprehension, as well as slowly building up writing and reading skills in the 2nd language. Reading is introduced using informative and interesting colorful books, with a lexicon of short sentences so students can slowly pick up intonation and fluency. Writing is done along with reading using teacher-created material, and/or worksheets, and students’ drawing booklets with subtitles. Students are also introduced to social language so they may begin to practice and feel they belong and are part of a group/class. Transitioning/Expanding: Students at this level of LA receive 90 minutes of stand alone instruction, using material that is more challenging to grow in the four modalities of LA. At this level students' need more syntactic structure to develop their listening, reading and writing skills to be able to communicate verbally, and make summaries, classify, compare and contrast, and define. To develop their reading and writing skills we use non-fiction texts, providing background knowledge, images, videos, graphic organizers, concept maps, and/or when possible, using realia to connect text-to-world; use of cognates for understanding. Integrated ENL: Per Part 154 teachers use different ENL strategies and methodologies. The four modalities of Language Acquisition are taught through core subjects to build academic language and content knowledge, with support in the home language, Spanish. Teachers provide translated materials to ELL students, as well as instruct them in Spanish so the ELL students can participate and feel that their input is recognized, and it is as important as the input of the rest of the whole class. An integrated, all-inclusive class enhances their learning experience, while they're going through the process of acquiring English. In our school, the highest levels of ELLs are Entering and Emerging, which contributes to lower writing scores. Writing is typically the last skill to develop in second-language acquisition. While students may quickly gain oral proficiency, mastering written expression requires deeper cognitive processing and structured practice. Entering and Emerging ELLs often develop conversational English faster than academic English, as writing demands formal sentence structures, grammar accuracy, and organization—skills that take longer to acquire. Additionally, many ELLs come from backgrounds where reading and writing in English are not regularly practiced at home, impacting their ability to internalize sentence structures and academic vocabulary needed for strong writing performance. Unlike speaking, which allows for immediate self-correction, writing requires planning, organizing, and revising, making it more cognitively demanding. Furthermore, some students' native languages have different writing conventions, making English writing rules challenging to grasp. 8.
