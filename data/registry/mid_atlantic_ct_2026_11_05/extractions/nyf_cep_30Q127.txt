Comprehensive Education Planning Overview of Comprehensive Education Planning Comprehensive Education Planning (CEP) is a structured, data-driven process that guides schools in assessing their needs, identifying areas for growth, setting strategic goals and implementing targeted actions to improve student outcomes. Collaboratively developed by the School Leadership Team (SLT) in accordance with the requirements of Chancellor’s Regulation A-655, the CEP meets New York State Education Department (NYSED) requirements under the Every Student Succeeds Act (ESSA) and aligns with NYC Public Schools’ (NYCPS) system-wide priorities. By engaging all stakeholders—school leaders, teachers, families, students and community members—schools can create a data-informed, equity- focused roadmap for student success and continuous improvement. Schools should focus their CEP on the five system-wide priorities below, connecting their planning to the data discussions and conversations with stakeholders held as part of the school’s needs assessment: Priority 1: All students learn to read well Priority 2: All students are physically and emotionally safe Priority 3: All students have a high-quality academic experience Priority 4: All students graduate college and career ready and have a strong plan and pathway to economic security Priority 5: All districts and schools are more inclusive and responsive for parents and families, including having more families choose NYC Public Schools The Education Planning Process The CEP follows an equity-driven, continuous improvement cycle, rooted in the system-wide priorities. This begins with a comprehensive needs assessment of the school’s most current data to identify priority needs and root causes. Next, the SLT establishes specific, measurable, attainable, relevant, and timely— SMART goal(s) aligned to each priority and determines targets to measure progress toward meeting the annual goals. The SLT and instructional leaders define key evidence-based key strategies, professional learning activities and parent, family and community empowerment efforts. They monitor the implementation of those strategies and activities and track progress through measurable targets. Then, the SLT aligns the school-based budget with human and instructional resources to support action plans. These include evidence based instructional strategies that address the educational needs of historically underserved populations and tackle structural inequities. Schools indicate how the plan addresses the needs of Multilingual/English Language Learners (ML/ELL) through development of the school’s Language Allocation Policy (LAP) & Title III Application (if applicable) and the Language Translation and Interpretation (LTI) Plan, that are required as part of the CEP. Schools also address the needs of Students with Disabilities (SWD) through the High Quality Inclusive & Accessible Learning for Students with Disabilities section of the plan. SLTs also develop a plan to improve attendance and reduce chronic absenteeism. Explanation/Background: Title I Schoolwide Program schools are expected to use the flexibility available to them to integrate services and programs with the aim of enhancing the entire educational program and helping all students reach proficient and advanced levels of achievement. In addition to coordinating and integrating services, Schoolwide Program schools may combine most Federal, State and local funds to provide those services. By consolidating funds from Federal, State, and local sources, a Schoolwide Program school can address its needs using all its available resources. This gives a school more flexibility in how it uses available resources to meet the identified needs of all its students. Consolidating funds in a Schoolwide Program means that a school treats the funds it is consolidating like they are a single “pool” of funds. In other words, the funds from the contributing programs in the school lose their individual identity and the school has one flexible pool of funds. The school uses funds from this consolidated Schoolwide pool to support any activity of the Schoolwide Program without regard to which program contributed the specific funds used for a particular activity – so long as the use of funds meets the intent and purposes of the programs from which the funds originate. To consolidate funding in a Schoolwide Program, the school does not literally need to combine funds in a single account or pool with its own accounting code. Rather, the word “pool” is used conceptually to convey that a Schoolwide Program school has the use of all consolidated funds available to it for the dedicated function of operating a Schoolwide Program without regard to the identity of those funds. Consolidating Federal funds in a Schoolwide Program has the following additional advantages: Consolidating Federal funds eases the requirements for accounting for funds from each specific program separately, because a Schoolwide school is not required to distinguish among funds received from different sources when accounting for their use. A school that consolidates Federal funds in its Schoolwide Program is not required to meet most of the statutory and regulatory requirements of the specific Federal programs included in the consolidation (e.g., semi-annual time and effort reporting for Title I). However, the school must ensure that it meets the intent and purposes of the Federal programs included in the consolidation so that the needs of the intended beneficiaries are met. 2. Rationale for Professional Development Describe the rationale for the professional development to be provided. For each activity, include: Qualitative and quantitative data analysis (e.g., patterns and trends identified) that support the need for the professional development, and DBN: 30Q127 School: P.S. 127 Aerospace Science Magnet School 211 of 235 Data sources (e.g., school survey teacher responses, in-house teacher surveys, teacher observation data) used to justify the professional development. The data analysis from the ELL Data Analysis Tool (EDAT) highlights a significant challenge for English Language Learners (ELLs), particularly in their ability to meet progress on the NYSESLAT assessments. Specifically, 59.6% of eligible test takers did not meet progress on the Spring 2024 NYSESLAT, which raises concerns about how effectively ELLs are acquiring language proficiency and academic skills, especially in writing. This data signals that a large portion of ELLs are struggling to meet expected progress benchmarks, and it underscores a need for targeted professional learning to address these gaps. Key Findings from the EDAT: Consistent Writing Struggles: Both the 2023 and 2024 test results consistently indicate that writing is an area of significant need for ELLs. This trend suggests that ELLs are facing persistent challenges in articulating their thoughts clearly and effectively in written form. Writing often requires not only language proficiency but also academic skills such as structuring ideas, using proper grammar, and demonstrating higher-level thinking through writing. This is compounded by the challenge of mastering content-area knowledge in English. Need for Tailored Instruction: The data suggests that the current instructional strategies may not be meeting the specific needs of ELLs, particularly in writing. If a majority of ELLs are not meeting progress benchmarks, this points to potential gaps in how teachers are providing support in areas such as vocabulary acquisition, sentence structure, writing conventions, and complex text comprehension—all of which are critical for academic writing. Disproportionate Gaps in Writing Skills: Since writing is a consistent area of weakness in both years of testing, it could indicate systemic or instructional issues that have not been adequately addressed over time. It may also suggest that while listening, speaking, and reading may show some progress, writing remains a persistent hurdle due to factors such as limited academic vocabulary, language transfer issues, and a lack of scaffolding in writing instruction. Why Professional Development is Crucial: To address these identified needs, professional learning for teachers is essential to ensure that they are equipped with the strategies, tools, and frameworks needed to help ELLs improve their writing skills. The following key areas would be critical for professional development: Culturally Responsive-Sustaining Education (CR-SE): Cultural Relevance: Teachers need to develop strategies to make writing instruction culturally relevant for ELLs. CR- SE emphasizes the importance of validating students' cultural backgrounds and lived experiences, helping ELLs feel seen and heard in the classroom. This can increase engagement and motivation to write, as students see the value in expressing themselves through writing. Building Connections: CR-SE also encourages making connections between students' home languages and cultures and the academic content they are learning. By fostering these connections, teachers can provide scaffolds that support ELLs in transferring skills from their home languages to English. Alignment with the New Language Arts Progressions: Comprehensive Writing Development: The New Language Arts Progressions offer a framework for developing writing skills across different grade levels. Teachers need professional learning on how to effectively use this DBN: 30Q127 School: P.S. 127 Aerospace Science Magnet School 212 of 235 framework to scaffold students' writing from simple sentences to more complex compositions, ensuring that they are meeting developmental milestones. Differentiated Support: These progressions also emphasize the need for differentiated instruction, where writing instruction can be tailored to ELLs' specific language proficiency levels, from beginner to advanced. Teachers need to be trained on how to assess where each student is in their writing development and adjust instruction accordingly. Hallmarks of Advanced Literacies: Writing as a Complex Process: Teachers need professional learning on how to teach writing as a complex, recursive process that includes prewriting, drafting, revising, and editing. For ELLs, this process requires an understanding of both language structure and content. Metacognitive Strategies: Teachers can benefit from training on how to teach metacognitive strategies that help ELLs reflect on their writing and self-monitor their language use. These strategies help students internalize the writing process and develop the ability to identify and correct their mistakes independently. Integration of Oral and Written Language: Given that writing is often linked to speaking and listening for ELLs, professional learning should address ways to integrate oral language development with writing. This could include oral brainstorming, peer discussions, and collaborative writing activities that help ELLs process ideas before translating them into written form. Assessment and Feedback: Formative Assessment: Teachers also need to be trained on how to use formative assessments effectively to monitor students’ writing progress and identify areas where additional support is needed. Regular feedback on writing can help ELLs understand what is working and where they need to improve. Building Writing Rubrics: Training teachers on how to use and develop writing rubrics that are specifically designed for ELLs can guide both instruction and assessment. These rubrics should be clear, culturally sensitive, and aligned to the specific challenges ELLs face when writing in English. Impact of Professional Learning: With professional learning aligned to CR-SE, the New Language Arts Progressions, and the Hallmarks of Advanced Literacies, teachers will be better prepared to implement differentiated, culturally responsive, and language-focused writing instruction. This will not only help ELLs improve their writing skills but also ensure that they make progress in other academic areas where writing is essential for success (e.g., science, social studies, and math). By addressing the specific needs identified in the EDAT report, such as the persistent challenges in writing, teachers can begin to close the achievement gap for ELLs, ultimately improving their ability to meet the progress expectations on the NYSESLAT in the future.
