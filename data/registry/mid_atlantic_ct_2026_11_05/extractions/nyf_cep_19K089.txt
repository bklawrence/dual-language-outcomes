School: P.S. 089 Cypress Hills 4 of 215 Section 1: Overview of Comprehensive Education Planning Section 1: Overview of Comprehensive Education Planning Overview of Comprehensive Education Planning Comprehensive Education Planning (CEP) is a structured, data-driven process that guides schools in assessing their needs, identifying areas for growth, setting strategic goals and implementing targeted actions to improve student outcomes. Collaboratively developed by the School Leadership Team (SLT) in accordance with the requirements of Chancellor’s Regulation A-655, the CEP meets New York State Education Department (NYSED) requirements under the Every Student Succeeds Act (ESSA) and aligns with NYC Public Schools’ (NYCPS) system-wide priorities. By engaging all stakeholders—school leaders, teachers, families, students and community members—schools can create a data-informed, equity- focused roadmap for student success and continuous improvement. Schools should focus their CEP on the five system-wide priorities below, connecting their planning to the data discussions and conversations with stakeholders held as part of the school’s needs assessment: Priority 1: All students learn to read well Priority 2: All students are physically and emotionally safe Priority 3: All students have a high-quality academic experience Priority 4: All students graduate college and career ready and have a strong plan and pathway to economic security Priority 5: All districts and schools are more inclusive and responsive for parents and families, including having more families choose NYC Public Schools The Education Planning Process The CEP follows an equity-driven, continuous improvement cycle, rooted in the system-wide priorities. This begins with a comprehensive needs assessment of the school’s most current data to identify priority needs and root causes. Next, the SLT establishes specific, measurable, attainable, relevant, and timely— SMART goal(s) aligned to each priority and determines targets to measure progress toward meeting the annual goals. The SLT and instructional leaders define key evidence-based key strategies, professional learning activities and parent, family and community empowerment efforts. They monitor the implementation of those strategies and activities and track progress through measurable targets. Then, the SLT aligns the school-based budget with human and instructional resources to support action plans. These include evidence based instructional strategies that address the educational needs of historically underserved populations and tackle structural inequities. Schools indicate how the plan addresses the needs of Multilingual/English Language Learners (ML/ELL) through development of the school’s Language Allocation Policy (LAP) & Title III Application (if applicable) and the Language Translation and Interpretation (LTI) Plan, that are required as part of the CEP. Schools also address the needs of Students with Disabilities (SWD) through the High Quality Inclusive & Accessible Learning for Students with Disabilities section of the plan. SLTs also develop a plan to improve attendance and reduce chronic absenteeism. The Cypress Hills Community School (CHCS) is a 3K-G8 community-based, school-wide dual language program led by the Principal in regular consultation with the School Leadership Team. This team includes parents, teachers, administrators, and members of the Cypress Hills Local Development Corporation (CHLDC), our partner community- based organization (CBO). Our vision is for all students to become fully bilingual and bicultural, deeply concerned about themselves, each other, their community, their environment, and the world. We aim to foster in the young people we serve, a capacity and propensity to take purposeful initiative*, so that they go forth seeking meaning and acting with purpose to achieve the conditions they desire in their own and others’ lives (*Definition of agency from Tom Vander Ark). CHCS serves a population that is 98% Hispanic and 96% qualifying for free or reduced lunch. Our English Language Learner (ELL) population makes up 49% of the K-8 student body, and 29% of our pre-K-8 student body are students with special needs. Our students with insecure housing make up 18% of the population. To address this specialized population's needs, CHCS developed a school-wide dual language program in 1997, a dual language Integrated Co- Teaching (ICT) program in 2008, and a Newcomers Middle School program in 2010. These initiatives established a strong, process-oriented approach to creating and implementing unique educational practices, with a focus the 3 goals of dual language education: bilingualism and biliteracy, high academic achievement in both languages, and sociocultural competence. CHCS continuously reflects on the implementation of our Dual Language Program and methods to enhance students' skills in both languages across content areas. For several years, we have designed and revised assessment tools to help teachers gather data demonstrating the acquisition of higher-order skills in both English and Spanish for all students. Weekly assessments and end-of-module assessments for the HMH Into Reading and Arriba la Lectura language arts curriculum for grades K-2, ELA Focusing-Question Tasks and end-of-module assessments for the Wit & Wisdom language arts curriculum for grades 3-8, and Math Learning Target Assessments as part of the Eureka Math Squared curriculum are administered every other week for grades K-8. These assessments are graded using criteria-based rubrics that measure students' knowledge, skills, and thinking. Otus enables teachers to manage learning and assessment digitally as needed; and to account for varied and multiple opportunities for students to work towards command of specific standards. Student work is uploaded in the platform or kept in classroom folders; and work products are graded using a standards-based rubric in the platform, offering a comprehensive system for tracking progress toward standards. Administrators and teachers also use Otus to reflect on teaching frequency and methods for assessing each standard and utilize student work for goal setting and differentiated learning. All K-8 students are assessed using the MAP Growth Assessment, a computer-adaptive reading assessment. By combining summative data, MAP Growth assessments, and ongoing formative assessments within integrated humanities units, teachers develop knowledge around reading behaviors and a deeper understanding of how to analyze student work/data to accelerate reading development. Throughout the year, consistent supports are provided to K-8 teachers to reinforce reading development, including cycles of support from the Early Reading Matters (ERM) Consultant and our school-based Literacy Coach, focusing on formative and summative data collected using Acadience and mClass to plan instruction in foundational skills and priority ELA standards. Grade- level teacher team meetings also help customize the K-2 HMH and 3-8 Wit & Wisdom ELA curricula, emphasizing learning activities that support progress in priority standards and implementing explicit instruction in writing. In grades K-3, teachers use Fundations, Heggerty, and Estrellita/Cancionero to improve students' phonemic awareness skills. Additionally, all K-3 teachers are trained in LETRS to enhance their pedagogy in the Science of Reading. The Spanish curriculum in 3-8 includes the use of Benchmark; it reflects grade level language arts standards, while emphasizing Spanish word-work and grammar, and the use of authentic Spanish texts to practice reading comprehension skills and to drive writing assignments. In math, teachers integrate productive struggle and visual models to support conceptual understanding. Number Talks are incorporated across grades K-8 to give voice to students and value various representations of mathematical thinking. This instructional routine encourages students to explain their thinking and build oral skills while developing concepts and fluency in math operations. Students collaboratively explore concepts through problem-based lessons with real-world scenarios, using tasks thoughtfully chosen with Universal Design for Learning in mind. This approach ensures entry points for a range of levels and opportunities to extend thinking to more sophisticated mathematical concepts. Discussions focus on student work and problem-solving strategies. Teachers also use digital platforms including iXL and Edmentum to ensure sufficient skills practice (or "reps") at individuals' levels. Students are grouped heterogeneously for concept exploration and more homogeneously for practice and enrichment during station teaching and targeted small group instruction. Our Learning Target Assessments (LTAs) are aligned with the standards and created by teachers using questions from the Eureka Math Squared mid- and end-line module assessments as well as questions from past NYS Math State Exams. These assessments address specific standards over 7-12 lessons, with student work maintained in the classroom and/or uploaded in Otus. The data gathered informs instruction throughout each unit. Furthermore, our school partners with ExpandEd to provide High Intensity Tutoring in ELA for students in grade 1 and 2, and in Math for students in grades 6-8. Tutors from outside organizations and classroom teachers meet with groups of no more than 4 students, three times a week, to provide them additional support. In addition, we have partnered with Young Audiences New York for over 20 years to provide additional arts programming beyond our regular music and art classes during the school day. Additional experiences including cooking, greenhouse/STEM, and dance with partner organizations further support students in building and activating prior knowledge, which translates into meaning-making in the classroom and memorable learning experiences. Explanation/Background: Title I Schoolwide Program schools are expected to use the flexibility available to them to integrate services and programs with the aim of enhancing the entire educational program and helping all students reach proficient and advanced levels of achievement. In addition to coordinating and integrating services, Schoolwide Program schools may combine most Federal, State and local funds to provide those services. By consolidating funds from Federal, State, and local sources, a Schoolwide Program school can address its needs using all its available resources. This gives a school more flexibility in how it uses available resources to meet the identified needs of all its students. Consolidating funds in a Schoolwide Program means that a school treats the funds it is consolidating like they are a single “pool” of funds. In other words, the funds from the contributing programs in the school lose their individual identity and the school has one flexible pool of funds. The school uses funds from this consolidated Schoolwide pool to support any activity of the Schoolwide Program without regard to which program contributed the specific funds used for a particular activity – so long as the use of funds meets the intent and purposes of the programs from which the funds originate. To consolidate funding in a Schoolwide Program, the school does not literally need to combine funds in a single account or pool with its own accounting code. Rather, the word “pool” is used conceptually to convey that a Schoolwide Program school has the use of all consolidated funds available to it for the dedicated function of operating a Schoolwide Program without regard to the identity of those funds. Consolidating Federal funds in a Schoolwide Program has the following additional advantages: Consolidating Federal funds eases the requirements for accounting for funds from each specific program separately, because a Schoolwide school is not required to distinguish among funds received from different sources when accounting for their use. A school that consolidates Federal funds in its Schoolwide Program is not required to meet most of the statutory and regulatory requirements of the specific Federal programs included in the consolidation (e.g., semi-annual time and effort reporting for Title I). However, the school must ensure that it meets the intent and purposes of the Federal programs included in the consolidation so that the needs of the intended beneficiaries are met. 2. For the purpose of this funding, immigrant students are defined as individuals who were not born in any U.S. state (this includes the District of Columbia and the Commonwealth of Puerto Rico); and have not been attending one or more schools in any one or more states for more than three full, academic years (the months need not be consecutive).
